# Content quality: the smallest sufficient structure

## Start with a reader's question

Write one sentence: "After reading this map, the user should be able to …" A learning map explains mechanisms and limits; a decision map explains options, evidence and constraints; an action map explains dependencies, responsibilities and acceptance. Organize primarily by source filename only for an explicit reference-index task.

Nodes should express claims, not act as quotation bins. "Latency covers warm inference only" is more useful than "Performance." Siblings should share a classification axis. Uneven branches and necessary single-child nodes are acceptable; symmetry is not a quality target.

## Place information by cognitive role

Visible branches answer the question. Expanded children supply reasons. Notes hold detail. Resource cards retain information that cannot be translated cheaply into text. A decisive condition belongs in the same visible layer as its claim; a hidden child cannot repair a misleading title.

Long derivations can move to notes; the conditions under which a result holds cannot disappear. Keep variable definitions in one canonical location rather than repeating them across many nodes.

## Three reduction tests

**Delete:** Would removing this change understanding, choice, execution or traceability? If not, remove it.

**Merge:** Are the entity, time, version, conditions and measurement definitions the same? Merge only when they are; retain the necessary source references. Similar names or identical bytes do not establish identical facts.

**Move down:** Must the reader keep this detail in view? If not, move it to notes or an attachment, without burying a decisive caveat.

Do not convert every sentence or every point of a plotted curve into a node. Give a repeated concept one primary home and multiple sources. Use labeled cross-links for real support, comparison, prerequisite or constraint relationships, not decoration. A tree is reading navigation, not a forced ontology of every causal relationship.

## High-risk details

Check negation, exceptions, conditions, units, denominators, sample mix, time windows, versions, uncertainty, missing data, target population, causal direction and unverified status. Do not turn association into causation, missing into zero, advice into fact or an estimate into a physical lower bound.

Align denominators, units, windows and measurement scope before comparing numbers. Mean, p95 and maximum are not interchangeable. A changed sample mix can make aggregate results misleading. Any recomputation needs source values, the expression and its assumptions.

## Reconcile before composing

For long tasks, read room summaries and question lists, collect limitations and counterexamples, then consolidate claims. Every required question needs either an answer or a visible explanation of insufficient material. No search hit is not proof that the collection contains no evidence; inspect coverage and unread regions.

First ask whether an apparent conflict arises from different entities, times or definitions. Preserve unresolved competing evidence. A newer document is not necessarily authoritative without a justified supersession relationship.

## Review as a reader

Can the visible map communicate the conclusion and its conditions? Can one branch explain its relationship to the root question? Can the reader reach a page, range, cell or timecode to check an important claim? Would removing half the nodes improve clarity without losing required evidence? If so, continue editing.

Do not certify quality using an arbitrary compression percentage, fixed branch count or weighted score. Look for actual defects: missed questions, distorted conclusions, repetition, broken evidence, unreadable resources, incorrect formulas or unusable layout. "Not ready for deployment" can be a complete, correct decision-map answer.
